The Business of Esports: How Competitive Gaming Became a Billion-Dollar Industry

By Priya Ramanathan · Culture & Markets

Ten years ago, watching strangers play video games for prize money was a niche hobby streamed to a few thousand viewers. Today, championship events fill arenas and pull in television-scale audiences, and the underlying economics have shifted just as dramatically.

Team ownership stakes now trade in ways that resemble traditional sports franchises, with venture funds and even a few legacy sports owners buying into top organizations at valuations that would have seemed absurd a decade ago. Sponsorship deals, once limited to energy drinks and gaming peripherals, now include banks, airlines, and luxury brands chasing a young, hard-to-reach audience.

Media rights are the newest frontier. Streaming platforms are bidding aggressively for exclusive broadcast rights to major tournaments, mirroring the rights wars that reshaped traditional sports broadcasting in the 2000s. Analysts covering the space now publish the same kind of revenue and franchise-valuation reports once reserved for the NBA or Premier League.

Not everyone in the scene is thrilled with the shift. Longtime players and community organizers worry that the influx of institutional money is pricing out the grassroots tournaments that built the culture in the first place.
